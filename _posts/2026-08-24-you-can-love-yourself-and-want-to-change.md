The self-love epidemic.

Yeah, I said what I said. It’s an epidemic.

Not in the way you think, though.

I am a huge believer in valuing yourself. Honestly, I am probably a little too obsessed with myself and likely a bit self-absorbed in many people’s eyes.

When I say self-love is an epidemic, I’m talking about the people who seem to think that wanting to improve, progress, or change means you don’t love yourself.

Just the other day, I realized one of my friends is this person.

Now, I should preface this story by saying that I am a very confident, self-assured person. At the same time, there are plenty of things I’d like to improve, and I am actively working on them: clearing up my skin, improving my sleep, eating healthier, working out, studying more for my career, and, yes, losing some fat.

Very standard self-improvement goals, in my opinion.

So I’m walking with my friend and we’re talking about how we need to hang out soon. She tells me there’s a party we should go to.

I’m not totally against going to a party, but when the party is an hour or more away, starts well past my bedtime, I don’t plan on drinking, and I only have one friend going, it’s not really something I want to do.

I’d rather plan a daytime hangout with that one friend.

So I tell her I don’t think I’ll be going.

And, of course, here come the questions:

- Friend: Why?

- Me: Because I go to bed early for the gym in the morning.

- F: You can leave early.

- M: It starts late, and it takes at least an hour to get home.

- F: You can crash at mine.

- M: But then I won’t be able to go to the gym before work, or eat my usual breakfast.

- F: You can miss one day at the gym and eat something out.

- M: I don’t want to miss one day.

- F: Why?

- M: Because… you know. I like staying on track. My career is progressing well, I’m working toward some goals, and I’m trying to lose some fat and get past a plateau.

- F: OMG. NO. DON’T SAY THAT.

- M: What?

- F: NO. STOP. STOP. YOU ALREADY ARE *BEAUTIFUL.*

- M: I didn’t say I wasn’t.

I just said I want to lose some fat.

And, frankly, the first step of losing fat is admitting that you have some fat to lose, creating a plan, and actually following it.

Okay, I didn’t actually get to finish saying that last part.

Because I’m sure you’ve met this person.

The second you say you want to lose a little weight, they freak out. They shut down the conversation. Suddenly you’re being told to love yourself, speak more kindly to yourself, accept your body, and stop trying to change.

Like… what?

I never said I hated myself.

I never said I didn’t think I was beautiful.

I am amazing. I am tenacious. I have strong values and a good head on my shoulders. I have a great personality, hobbies, goals, and a life I’m genuinely excited about.

Honestly, I am rather obsessed with myself.

And all of that can be true while I still want to improve myself.

In fact, dare I say it:

I want to change because I care about myself.

Going to bed instead of staying out late.

Waking up early to work on myself instead of sleeping in and bedrotting.

Putting myself out there for new opportunities and building my career.

Choosing healthy, homemade meals instead of fast food that leaves me feeling sluggish.

Working out and losing fat so I can move more freely, feel more confident, and feel stronger in my body.

Putting down the drinks and opting for water.

Saying no.

None of those things require self-hatred.

If anything, I would argue that being able to choose what is best for you, your goals, and your life requires an immense amount of confidence.

Choosing to do the hard thing instead of the easy thing.

Choosing the long-term reward over the immediate gratification.

Being willing to disappoint someone else because you know what you want.

Having the discipline to say no.

That doesn’t sound like someone who hates herself to me.

That sounds like someone who believes she is worth the effort.

And honestly, maybe the biggest hot take of all:

Sometimes self-love becomes a crutch.

Not because loving yourself is bad. It isn’t.

But because we’ve started confusing self-love with never being uncomfortable.

Never changing.

Never pushing yourself.

Never admitting that there are things you could do better.

Never asking more of yourself.

And that is a problem.

Because comfort is lovely, but comfort is not where you grow.

Complacency is one of the biggest dream killers there is. If you convince yourself that wanting more means you don’t appreciate what you already have, you can very easily talk yourself out of becoming the person you’re capable of being.

You can love who you are today and still be excited about who you’re becoming.

You can think you’re beautiful and still want to change your body.

You can be proud of yourself and still want to get better.

You can accept yourself and still have higher standards for yourself.

You can love yourself and still want more.

Those things aren’t contradictions.

They’re probably the healthiest combination there is.

-love, the girl in 3C
